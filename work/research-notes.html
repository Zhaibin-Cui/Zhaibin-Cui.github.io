Data determines the learning problem.

My starting point is the distribution the model actually learns from. Quality filtering, domain balance, repeated exposure and sequence construction each change that distribution. A cleaner corpus is not automatically a more informative one: filtering can also remove rare concepts, difficult examples and useful variation.

I am interested in separating knowledge coverage, sample efficiency and compositional generalization. One way to make this tractable is to construct data with known factors of variation, vary a single factor, then test both familiar combinations and held-out compositions. The question becomes which structure the model learned, rather than whether a global score moved.

DoReMi treats mixture selection as an optimization problem; Olmo 3 makes data curation and training stages inspectable. These examples inform the questions I ask about data recipes and cross-scale transfer. In my own work, the industrial setting supplies realistic constraints, while MiniTrainSys provides a controlled environment for probing explanations.

Architecture is a hypothesis about computation.

I read an architecture by asking what information it retains, how information moves, and where computation is allocated. Attention provides content-dependent access to context. Recurrent state-space layers maintain a compressed state. Sparse experts separate total parameter capacity from the computation activated for a token. These are complementary choices, and hybrid models can combine them.

The interesting consequences lie beyond the block diagram: positional structure affects extrapolation; normalization and residual paths affect optimization; expert routing affects specialization and load balance; attention design affects the cost of carrying context into generation. Capacity, inductive bias and execution cost need to be considered together.

DeepSeek-V3 provides a concrete study of compressed attention state, expert computation and training efficiency. State-space duality offers a mathematical view of the relation between attention and recurrence. I use such comparisons to formulate ablations—what should change if a component is responsible for the observed behavior?

Storing information is not the same as using it.

A model can contain information that a particular prompt fails to retrieve. Conversely, successful output can come from shortcuts in the data rather than the intended reasoning process. I therefore distinguish acquisition, representation, extraction and use when thinking about knowledge in neural networks.

My controlled-model work begins with data whose facts and relations are known, then compares readout behavior across training and architectural choices. Changing the representation, the query form or the required composition helps identify where a failure arises. I regard a probe as evidence about accessibility, not by itself a causal explanation of what the model computes.

This interest is closely aligned with the Physics of Language Models program. The aim is to make an ambiguous behavior experimentally tractable: define what should be learned, isolate a cause, and test whether the explanation survives a new task or scale.

Reasoning needs tests that can distinguish explanations.

A convincing reasoning result must separate memorized patterns from reusable procedures. I am interested in hidden computation, intermediate representations, error recovery and the relation between training objectives and inference-time behavior. My ongoing MiniTrainSys studies extend a completed factual-readout setup toward these questions.

I would test generalization along distinct axes: unfamiliar compositions, longer dependency chains, altered surface forms and shifts in difficulty. More generated tokens are not, by themselves, evidence of a better mechanism. Accuracy, failure patterns and inference cost should be read together, with controls that distinguish additional computation from a better search or verification strategy.

The experiment should make the competing explanations explicit before it is run. That discipline comes naturally from experimental physics: define the observable, change the relevant variable, and check the conditions under which the conclusion stops holding.

A research idea needs an executable experiment.

I build the tools needed to move quickly from a hypothesis to a comparison. MiniTrainSys connects readable model implementations, distributed training and reproducible measurements. Continuum brings model sessions, agent collaboration, code and experiment access into one working environment. In PaGE, architecture and data work meet distillation and practical inference.

That experience shapes my understanding of model systems. Training memory includes parameters, gradients, optimizer state and activations; the useful parallelization strategy depends on both compute and communication. Inference has a different balance: prefill and decoding expose different bottlenecks, while cache layout, sequence length and batching affect memory traffic and latency.

The purpose of systems work is to make stronger research possible. I want comparisons whose results remain interpretable when an implementation, scale or execution environment changes—and tools that make those comparisons inexpensive to repeat.

From understanding models to learning about the physical world.

My broader interest is in models that form useful representations of environments and use them to predict, reason and act. Language-model mechanisms, spatial representations and world models are connected by a common question: what structure must a model learn to generalize beyond the situations it has seen?

My contribution to a research team combines large-scale pretraining experience, controlled algorithm studies, visual modeling and the ability to build across software and physical systems. Quantum experiments developed my grounding in physics, measurement and full-stack instrumentation; I bring that same attention to causal explanations and working implementations to foundation-model research.

I welcome research roles and collaborations in foundation models, multimodal learning, data and training research, and world models. The most interesting conversations start with a concrete question we can turn into an experiment.
