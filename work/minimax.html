Research across data, models and scale

My industrial work combines large-scale training experience with pretraining research: scaling-law analysis, data-mixture and document-packing studies, evaluation development, and contributions to the team’s data pipelines. I participate in experimental design, implementation and analysis within the existing training stack.

The central question is how to turn a promising local result into a reliable training decision. A lower loss at one checkpoint is useful evidence, but I also examine capability changes, recipe rankings across scales, training stage and the cost of achieving the improvement.

Data composition as a modeling decision

Pretraining data is a distribution to design, not simply a volume to accumulate. I study how source mixtures, quality strata, domain coverage and sequence construction alter the learning problem. Document packing and sampling interact with the context seen by the model, so they belong in the experimental design alongside the corpus recipe.

My work includes comparing candidate data strategies through controlled training and evaluation, and contributing the required changes to shared data-processing and training-input pipelines. I connect corpus-level choices to model behavior rather than treating a filter score or an aggregate metric as the objective.

Scaling behavior and experiment selection

I work with multi-scale training experiments to assess whether conclusions from smaller runs remain useful at larger scales. Scaling-law analysis provides a framework for reasoning about model capacity, data and compute; held-out comparisons test whether that framework actually predicts a decision-relevant result.

I distinguish a recipe’s learning efficiency from its eventual performance, and a fitted trend from evidence of transfer. This perspective guides which hypotheses deserve more training budget and which need a better control, a different evaluation slice or a longer training horizon.

Training dynamics and capability diagnosis

My evaluation work connects pretraining progress to downstream knowledge and reasoning behavior. I compare aggregate trends with capability-specific results, inspect changes across training stages, and use evaluation consistency to separate model effects from differences in the experimental setup.

Participation in large-scale GPU runs also makes the system constraints concrete: memory, communication, data delivery and numerical behavior all affect the experiments that can be run. I approach training efficiency together with convergence and capability, rather than interpreting throughput in isolation.

Architecture informs the questions I ask

Beyond the immediate experimental scope, I study how architecture changes the relationship between information storage, computation and inference cost. My interests include attention and long-context representations, sparse expert routing, recurrent/state-space computation, and the interaction between auxiliary training objectives and reasoning behavior.

I connect this reading to controlled model studies in MiniTrainSys. Public reports such as DeepSeek-V3, Olmo 3 and NVIDIA’s Nemotron series provide concrete comparisons of architecture, data and optimization choices. The research notes linked below develop this perspective; they do not imply that I implemented those systems at MiniMax.

Experimental conditions & contribution scope
The training infrastructure and data pipelines are team-developed. My contribution is pretraining research, experiments and implementation within those systems.

Questions that guide the work.

How much of a capability comes from data coverage, how much from the training objective, and how much from the model’s computational structure? When a recipe improves, does the gain survive a change in scale, budget or evaluation setting?

I use these questions to connect industrial experimentation with my independent model studies. My research notebook develops the connections between architectural inductive biases, knowledge representations, reasoning and the systems needed to investigate them.
