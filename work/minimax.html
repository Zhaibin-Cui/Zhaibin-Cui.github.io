I work on pretraining algorithms at MiniMax and participate in training on large GPU clusters. My work covers scaling ladders, scaling-law analysis, task benchmarks, bits-per-byte (BPB) evaluation, document packing, data mixtures, and data-pipeline development. Large-scale pretraining and scaling ladders

I participate in pretraining on large GPU clusters and compare data recipes and training configurations on small, intermediate, and larger models. A scaling ladder allows lower-cost experiments to screen a candidate recipe before larger-scale validation. Comparisons record model size, training tokens, batch size, and learning-rate schedule.

For scaling-law studies, I examine how loss varies with parameter count, data volume, and compute budget. I compare trends across recipes, distinguish fitting observed runs from predicting held-out scales, and check whether a recipe’s advantage is limited to a particular model size or training stage.

I track training and validation loss, benchmark scores, effective-token throughput, training stability, relative recipe performance, and extrapolation error. Chinchilla and OLMo-ladder provide public methodological references; they do not describe the company’s configurations or results.

Task benchmarks and BPB evaluation

I contribute to pretraining benchmarks and evaluation workflows for comparing checkpoints, data domains, and training recipes. Knowledge and reasoning benchmarks measure task performance, while held-out loss measures prediction of a specified text distribution. I use both to assess a training recipe.

Bits per byte (BPB) divides the summed negative log-likelihood of scored tokens, expressed in bits, by the UTF-8 byte count of the corresponding text. For natural-log NLL, BPB = NLL / (ln 2 × byte count). Context-only tokens and unscored padding are excluded from the scored text used in the numerator and denominator.

BPB comparisons need consistent text, normalization, context windows, boundary handling, scoring masks, and aggregation. Byte normalization reduces the unit mismatch between tokenizers with different granularities. It does not remove tokenizer or evaluation-protocol effects, so domain-level losses and task scores still matter.

BPB = −∑t ∈ S ln pθ(xt | x<t)ln 2 · BS

S denotes the scored tokens and B_S the UTF-8 byte count of the corresponding original text. Corpus BPB uses total negative log-likelihood in bits divided by total scored bytes, rather than an unweighted average of document BPB values.

Document packing, data mixtures, and quality tiers

I compare document-packing strategies to understand how document boundaries, sequence lengths, and padding affect compute utilization and the training objective. When documents share a sequence, attention masks, position IDs, and loss masks determine the available context and the positions that contribute to loss.

I use domain classification, quality tiers, bucketed sampling, data mixtures, and augmentation to compare corpus composition and presentation. Public Nemotron work provides references for quality classification, deduplication, and synthetic data. The value of a particular recipe is tested through training and evaluation at comparable budgets.

Alongside model performance, I track effective-token fraction, padding fraction, sequence-length distribution, throughput, and domain-level validation loss. These measurements help distinguish gains from processing more non-padding tokens from gains due to data content. Mixture comparisons also check configured sampling weights against the actual exposure of each source during training.

Pretraining data-pipeline development and integration

I contribute to corpus preprocessing, tokenization, sharding, document packing, and data loading within an existing training platform. I help integrate candidate data strategies and verify that the constructed samples and the data consumed by training match the intended design.

Integration checks cover token and sample counts, shard and index consistency, document boundaries, and masking behavior, followed by verification of the distribution consumed during training. This helps distinguish the effect of a data strategy from changes introduced by sample construction, loading, or evaluation.

I also track data-preparation and loading throughput, realized sampling distributions, and input consistency, and keep data versions linked to training settings and checkpoints. Once a candidate recipe is integrated, controlled pretraining runs and benchmark evaluation test its effect.

Cross-scale comparisons and validation

I test whether a smaller-model result persists at a larger scale by comparing model size, training budget, data recipe, and checkpoint stage together. This helps identify effects tied to a particular budget, stage of training, or evaluation setup.

Loss and domain-level comparisons keep other variables as consistent as possible and distinguish token budgets from compute budgets. Compute accounting depends on architecture, sequence length, and execution policy, with the scope of forward, backward, activation recomputation, and optimizer updates stated explicitly. In MoE, per-token active computation differs from state storage for all parameters. Attention cost also depends on sequence structure, masks, and implementation, beyond parameter count and total tokens.

FLOP accounting distinguishes estimates of model operations from execution that includes additional work, such as activation recomputation. Throughput reports specify processed or effective tokens and global or per-GPU scope. Wall-clock time also includes exposed communication, data loading, and synchronization. With those definitions aligned, I compare loss trends, task-level differences, and reversals in recipe rankings, and test extrapolation on scales or training stages excluded from the fit.

These comparisons help decide which experiments to scale up, which controls are missing, and how to handle trade-offs between metrics. OLMo-ladder, which models intermediate loss features separately from task performance, is a public reference for this approach.
